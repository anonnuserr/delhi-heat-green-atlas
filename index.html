Where would trees cool Delhi the most?

A satellite study of which kind of green actually cools Delhi, which dense neighbourhoods need it most, and where open land to plant canopy already sits next to them.

SummaryAt a glance
Abstract

Using Landsat 8/9 imagery from three pre-monsoon seasons (2023–2025), this study compares land surface temperature across tree cover, grassland and built-up land in Delhi. Greenness and surface temperature were negatively correlated in every season (r = −0.52 to −0.61). Within the same 1 km neighbourhoods, tree cover was the coolest land type in 77–89 % of 764 squares, while open grassland was a median 1.3–1.6 °C hotter than trees and slightly hotter than built-up land. A greening-priority index combining heat, lack of vegetation and population points to dense settlements in west, north-west, north-east and south Delhi and the old city. Of about 3,750 ha of open grassland hotter than the average built-up surface, about 710 ha lies within 500 m of a priority hotspot. For cooling, nature-based solutions in Delhi should favour tree canopy over open green space, and the land to plant it is scarcest where the need is greatest.

Key results

ContextWhy it matters

Green cover as a nature-based solution for heat

Delhi's pre-monsoon heat is a growing public-health problem. A 2026 assessment by the Centre for Science and Environment found that over 70 % of the city's area is persistently heat-stressed.[4] Dense built-up areas absorb and re-radiate more heat than vegetated land, and heat exposure falls hardest on people with the fewest ways to escape it.[5]

Trees cool their surroundings through shade and through evapotranspiration, the water their leaves release. Using ecosystems deliberately to meet a societal challenge like this, while also benefiting people and biodiversity, is what the UN Environment Assembly defined in 2022 as a nature-based solution (NbS).[1][2] The IUCN Global Standard sets out eight criteria for designing and verifying such interventions.[3]

Not all green cools equally. Across 58 studies worldwide, trees lowered local air temperature two to three times more than grass, green roofs or green walls.[9] A satellite study of European cities found treeless green space two to four times less effective than trees at lowering surface temperature, and in dry southern regions often warmer than the built-up fabric itself.[7] Whether that holds in Delhi's semi-arid climate, at the scale of individual neighbourhoods, is the gap this study addresses.

MethodHow it was done

From satellite scenes to a priority map

  1. Collect clear Landsat scenesLandsat 8 and 9 Collection 2 Level 2 images over Delhi, 15 April – 15 June of 2023, 2024 and 2025, keeping scenes under 20 % cloud (10, 16 and 13 scenes).[11]
  2. Remove bad pixelsMask fill, cloud, cirrus, cloud shadow and physically implausible temperatures (outside 10–75 °C) using the QA band.
  3. Compute greenness and heatNDVI from the red and near-infrared bands, and land surface temperature from thermal band 10; take each pixel's median across the season.[15]
  4. Keep comparable landKeep only tree cover, grassland and built-up land from ESA WorldCover, so ploughed fields, bare soil and water do not distort the comparison.[12]
  5. Measure the relationshipSample random pixels each season (about 2,500 valid); compute Pearson correlation, a linear fit, and mean temperature for each land-cover type.
  6. Test within neighbourhoodsSplit Delhi into 1 km squares. Where a square holds at least 1.8 ha each of trees, grassland and built-up land (764 squares), compare the three inside that square, then take medians across squares.
  7. Score greening priorityOn built-up land only, rescale heat, lack of vegetation and population to 0–1 within Delhi and average them; map the top 10 % as hotspots and outline the largest clusters.[13]
  8. Find room to plantMark open grassland hotter than the average built-up surface that lies within 500 m of a priority hotspot, and total it around each of the largest clusters.

Findings

Q1 · Relationship

Greener ground was consistently cooler

In every season, greenness and surface temperature were moderately to strongly negatively correlated (r between −0.52 and −0.61). Each +0.1 rise in NDVI went with a surface roughly 0.9 to 1.1 °C cooler.

Q2 · Type of green

Canopy cools. Open grass does not.

City-wide, tree cover was the coolest land in all three seasons and grassland the hottest. Inside the same 1 km squares the gap shrinks but holds: trees were coolest in 77–89 % of squares, and grassland ran a median 1.3–1.6 °C hotter than trees. The local tree–built gap is about half the city-wide one, so part of the city-wide difference reflects where trees grow; the local figure is the conservative estimate.

Q3 · Where to act

Priority concentrates in dense settlements

The top 10 % of built-up land (priority score 0.80 or higher) forms clusters mainly in west, north-west, north-east and south Delhi and the old city. The largest single cluster covers about 970 ha around Uttam Nagar – Bindapur.

Q4 · Room to plant

Most hot open land lies away from the people who need shade

Delhi has about 3,750 ha of open grassland hotter than the average built-up surface (41.6 °C in 2025). Only about one-fifth of it lies within 500 m of any priority hotspot, and about 87 ha lies next to the ten largest clusters, which together cover about 2,790 ha of dense settlement. The most room is near Uttam Nagar – Bindapur, Bhalswa, Mustafabad – Gokulpuri and Jahangirpuri. Converting these patches to shaded canopy is a concrete first step; elsewhere, street trees and shade inside the settlements will have to do most of the work.

Robustness and comparison

Consistent across seasons, and in line with dry-climate studies

2024 was the hottest of the three seasons in this data (built-up land averaged 46.2 °C), yet the order stayed the same every year: trees coolest, grassland hottest, built-up land in between. The correlation and slope also stayed within a narrow band.

The size of the tree effect fits what is known about dry climates. During hot extremes, a satellite study of European cities found tree areas 8–12 K cooler than the urban fabric in central Europe but only 0–4 K cooler in the drier south, where treeless green space was often warmer than built-up land, as it was here.[7] A 2025 review covering 110 cities likewise found that tree cooling depends strongly on background climate and urban form.[10] Delhi's 0.9–1.2 °C local tree–built gap falls within the range reported for dry southern Europe. Dry pre-monsoon conditions also explain why open land heats up so much: by day, Delhi's surface is cooler than its drier rural surroundings.[6]

ImplicationsFor nature-based solutions

What greening would have to get right

Type of green

Open grassland ran hotter than concrete in the dry season while tree cover was coolest. Cooling needs canopy and shade; a study in Madison, USA, found the largest gains once canopy passed about 40 % at roughly city-block scale.[8]

Water

Evaporative cooling needs water, and tree cooling weakens where soils are dry.[7] In a water-stressed city, drought-tolerant native species and rainwater use matter as much as planting numbers.

Space

The hottest, densest areas have the least free land. Converting nearby hot open grassland to canopy avoids competing for built space; inside settlements, street trees and school grounds remain the realistic options.

Fairness

Putting population in the priority score asks who is exposed, not only where it is hot, so greening reaches people with the fewest alternatives to shade.

Upkeep

Saplings that die in the first summer cool nothing. Survival, maintenance budgets and local stewardship decide the real outcome.

Evidence loop

The same satellite method can check, season after season, whether greened areas actually got cooler.

LimitsRead the results with these in mind
  • Surface, not air, temperature. Landsat measures how hot the ground and roofs are in the late morning (about 10:30 am IST). Air temperature and night-time heat, which affect health most, can differ.
  • Association, not proof of cause. Green areas differ from built-up areas in other ways too, such as building height, materials and nearby water. The same-neighbourhood test reduces but does not remove this.
  • Pixels are not independent. Neighbouring pixels resemble each other, so p-values from thousands of samples overstate certainty. The study reports effect sizes and checks consistency across three seasons instead.
  • The priority score involves choices. Heat, greenness and population are weighted equally. Other weights would shift the hotspots.
  • “Grassland” is a broad class. In ESA WorldCover it covers any land dominated by grasses or herbs, which in Delhi includes dry open grounds, scrub and floodplain grass as well as parks. Irrigated lawns may behave differently.
  • Not all open land is plantable. Some hot grassland near hotspots may be floodplain, protected or otherwise unsuitable for trees; sites need checking on the ground. Totals around the ten clusters can also double-count land where two clusters lie within 1 km of each other.
  • Data dates differ. Land cover is from 2021 and population is a modelled 2025 estimate. Neighbourhood names mark each cluster's centre and were checked on Google Maps, but a large cluster can extend into neighbouring localities.

Next steps

  • Add night-time temperature (MODIS) to capture heat that stays after sunset.
  • Measure how far the cooling of large parks such as the Ridge reaches into surrounding streets.
  • Add vulnerability layers (age, housing type, access to cooling) to the priority score.
  • Screen candidate planting sites for ownership, flood risk and soil, then track planted sites over time.
